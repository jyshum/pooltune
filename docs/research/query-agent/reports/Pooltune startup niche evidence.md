# Focus Pooltune on avoidable query slowdowns

**The strongest provisional niche is ongoing query-performance care for small Supabase SaaS teams without a dedicated database specialist.** The proposed job is to keep important application queries fast as stored data, customer usage, and releases change, using permitted repairs and measured outcomes. The eight capability families fit the broader theme of PostgreSQL performance operations, but they do not define a precise customer promise by themselves. Published startup accounts support the existence of avoidable database work, resource interference, and difficult diagnosis; they also reveal problems that local tuning cannot fix. This supports a focused product hypothesis, not proven customer demand or reliable autonomous execution. Jared's lack of personal production incidents does not prevent building an honest, useful engineering project around documented cases and controlled workloads.

Prepared November 12, 2026. This is research and product brainstorming, not an approved build plan. No customer project was accessed, no repairs were executed, and no Pooltune benchmark results or savings are claimed.

## Startup evidence separates repairable waste from architectural limits

**Chatbase illustrates one workload slowing another.** In a vendor-published customer story, Supabase describes heavy Metabase reporting queries competing with ordinary application work. Scheduled reporting reached disk limits even though average activity appeared less concerning. The described response combines expensive-query investigation, greater disk capacity, and a separate read replica for analytics. Some steps are presented as a plan, so the story does not establish completed improvements or bill savings. Its useful lesson is that a growing startup can suffer from the interaction between workloads, rather than simply having “too many users.” It also shows why more capacity and query optimization can both be appropriate. ([Supabase customer story](https://supabase.com/customers/chatbase))

**A firsthand index repair shows a narrower opportunity.** Engineer Aditya Jindal describes an investigations page taking 30–40 seconds while a different, frequently executed quota check scanned a 1.7-million-row table. The existing index did not fit that query. A corrected index reduced a shown execution from 605 milliseconds to 0.048 milliseconds; the author reports the investigations page improving to roughly two seconds before further changes. This is a firsthand engineering account with query plans, but the company identity and representativeness are not established. The important product lesson is that the slow screen was affected by other unnecessary database work. Pooltune would need to identify that relationship, rather than optimize only the query named by the user. ([Engineer account](https://adityajindal.dev/writing/postgres-index-win))

**incident.io shows why the apparent symptom can suggest the wrong repair.** Its 2023 account describes intermittent waits approaching 20 seconds for database connections. Multiple changes did not eliminate the problem. The eventual fix removed unnecessary transactions around certain Slack interactions; the author reported four subsequent months without those timeouts. A transaction groups database operations, and keeping one open unnecessarily can keep a connection occupied. This case supports an agent that recognizes application-level causes and declines an unjustified pool expansion. It does not establish that Pooltune could make the necessary code change unattended. ([Company postmortem](https://incident.io/blog/database-performance))

**Lago marks the architectural boundary.** Its official engineering wiki describes event analytics and ingestion placing heavy load on the application. The team moved event analytics to ClickHouse while keeping PostgreSQL for other data. This was a separation of fundamentally different work, with no controlled latency or dollar-saving figures in the account. Pooltune's local repair catalog cannot honestly promise to perform that entire architectural transition. Recognizing the boundary is part of competent diagnosis. ([Lago engineering account](https://github.com/getlago/lago/wiki/Using-Clickhouse-to-scale-an-events-engine))

These examples establish real problems, not their prevalence among early-stage startups. No representative survey, typical database budget, or willingness to grant autonomous production access was established. They nevertheless give Pooltune a stronger starting point than a generic promise to “handle scale”: find avoidable work, repair eligible causes, and explain when the necessary remedy lies elsewhere.

## Choose a recurring customer job before an eight-feature promise

The proposed customer is **a small team running a Supabase-backed SaaS product with real users and important queries that change in cost over time**. The trigger is worsening behavior after data growth, a large customer arriving, an import, or a release. The outcome is verified recovery of those queries while preserving results, access rules, and other important work. A release can trigger the same product without turning release protection into a separate headline.

This is narrower than managing all database infrastructure. It excludes choosing a new database architecture, solving provider outages, guaranteeing unlimited traffic, or preventing every compute upgrade. It is also more concrete than “an AI agent with eight tools.” The customer values the recovered workload and reduced investigation effort; the tool count is an engineering detail.

The strongest alternative is **maintenance for update-heavy Supabase tables**: imports, queues, synchronization, and similar work repeatedly create old record versions requiring cleanup. Selected-table authority and bounded maintenance fit unattended execution relatively well. However, PostgreSQL already automates cleanup, and its documentation says the native process is sufficient for many installations. A permanent setting adjustment can also eliminate recurring intervention. This alternative should win only if evidence shows continuing useful work beyond the native behavior. ([PostgreSQL routine maintenance](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/17/routine-vacuuming.html))

A still narrower “stop reports from slowing the app” product has a tangible customer story in Chatbase. Its weakness is that the best solution can quickly become a replica, different routing, or a separate analytics system. Those exceed the current unattended repair scope. **Query-performance care is therefore the best provisional fit to the existing research, while maintenance is the strongest alternative for tighter operational boundaries.** This is a product judgment, not a measured market ranking.

Supabase already provides advisors and documents a read-only performance-agent workflow for blockers, regressions, and recommendations. Pooltune's proposed contribution is completing an eligible repair and verifying its effect. Another report saying “this query is slow” would overlap heavily with existing tools. ([Supabase performance-agent workflow](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/observability/automate-with-agents/performance))

## Keep the catalog, but give each family a clear role

The current catalog contains eight **families**, each with multiple possible operations and different permissions. Keeping research in the catalog does not require releasing every operation automatically.

| Existing family | Plain-English purpose | Fit to the proposed niche |
|---|---|---|
| Planner statistics | Improve the information PostgreSQL uses to choose how to execute a query. | Core when data changes cause poor execution choices. |
| Index design and lifecycle | Give queries an efficient way to locate records without unnecessary searching. | Core, with write and storage costs included in verification. |
| Table and index maintenance | Address demonstrated cleanup or physical-storage problems. | Supporting repair when the problem affects important queries; core in the maintenance alternative. |
| Scoped RPC execution | Adjust execution for a selected database function, such as its working-memory allowance. | Conditional support for a measured problem in an enrolled function. |
| Existing-pool control | Adjust permitted connection ceilings where the limit is genuinely responsible. | Peripheral. Native pooling already handles part of the problem, and waits can originate elsewhere. |
| Scheduled work | Move eligible background work within approved time windows. | Supporting when a job demonstrably interferes with important queries. |
| WAL and checkpoint policy | Adjust how database changes are recorded and written to durable storage. | Weakest fit for the first query-focused release; keep as research until a concrete target case justifies it. |
| Reviewed code and policy optimization | Prepare changes to queries, database functions, or access rules. | Relevant when operational changes cannot fix the cause, but a reviewed patch is a different promise from unattended maintenance. |

The niche does not demand one tool. It demands one recognizable customer outcome. All eight could eventually support that outcome, but **passing existing tests is insufficient justification for adding a family**. It should complete useful cases the smaller product cannot handle, without unacceptable harm or investigation cost.

## The harness must prove useful repairs and correct restraint

The user's concern about the harness is central. Adding tools does not retrain or distill the model; it changes the information and choices available during a task. The **harness** is the surrounding software that supplies evidence, controls actions, checks results, and handles interruption or failure. Eight narrow operations can be easier to constrain than one unrestricted SQL tool. Conversely, more operations introduce more situations and combinations to validate. Anthropic explicitly treats agent evaluation as evaluation of the model and harness together, and distinguishes the agent's success claim from the actual final state. ([Agent evaluation guidance](https://www.anthropic.com/research/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents), [Tool design guidance](https://www.anthropic.com/research/writing-tools-for-agents))

For Pooltune, a useful test starts with a symptom and imperfect evidence, not a supplied diagnosis and an obvious button to press. The agent must identify a genuine opportunity, perform an allowed repair, and improve useful work under comparable conditions. It must also leave healthy databases alone, distinguish similar symptoms with different causes, preserve returned data and access rules, and recognize missing evidence or permissions. Repeated runs must avoid duplicate indexes, unnecessary maintenance, and settings that oscillate between values.

**The measurement must match the promise.** PostgreSQL query statistics expose execution aggregates, row counts, buffer activity, and temporary disk activity. They do not provide the full distribution of user-visible page response times. A lower average database execution time cannot prove that nearly all users now receive a fast page. Indexes can also make reads faster while increasing write work. Verification therefore needs completed work, failures, affected reads and writes, and representative inputs, rather than one faster query or a lower CPU chart. ([Statement statistics](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/17/pgstatstatements.html), [Index tradeoffs](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/17/indexes-intro.html))

Compare a smaller action set with additional families on held-out cases and repeated runs. Examine completed eligible repairs, harmful changes, correct decisions to do nothing, and recognition of unsupported cases by family. Synthetic cases establish mechanics and expose failures; they do not establish customer demand. No such Pooltune comparison has yet been run, so there is no evidence that eight families preserve or reduce reliability.

Supabase supplies real inputs through metrics and database inspection, including resource pressure, costly queries, blockers, and maintenance state. These establish feasibility, not automatic permission or sufficient business context. Actual project access, version support, and authority to perform each operation still require verification. ([Metrics](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/observability/metrics), [Database inspection](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/observability/inspect))

## Frame cost and the interview story honestly

More compute sometimes is the right solution. Optimization can recover wasted capacity or delay an upgrade, but **lower CPU use does not automatically lower a fixed-size Supabase project's bill**. Compute charges depend on selected resources and running time. A savings claim needs an actual smaller configuration, avoided-upgrade comparison, or measured reduction in a billed resource. None of the selected cases establishes a typical startup bill or a general savings percentage. ([Supabase compute billing](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/platform/manage-your-usage/compute))

Angel's references to calls, transactions, and a pooler do not identify MongoDB or any particular bottleneck. PostgreSQL supports transactions and JSON documents, and schema design matters there too. An illustrative call application could keep accounts and billing records in PostgreSQL while storing audio files in object storage. That is an example, not a claim about his system. ([PostgreSQL transactions](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/17/tutorial-transactions.html), [PostgreSQL JSON](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/17/datatype-json.html), [Supabase Storage](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/storage))

A defensible current interview description is: **“I’m designing Pooltune, an open-source agent for small Supabase teams that investigates important queries getting slower as data and workloads change. The focus is bounded database repairs with measured verification, including knowing when an application or architecture change is required.”** After implementation and evaluation, replace intentions with actual supported operations and observed results. There is no need to imply personal startup-scale production experience.

## Conclusion

Pooltune can have a specific niche while retaining several repair capabilities. The narrowing decision should govern whose work it protects, which outcomes it measures, and which changes it can finish reliably. That is more meaningful than choosing a maximum number of tools.

The next product evidence should come from published incidents, startup conversations, and representative controlled workloads, rather than asking Jared to supply production problems he has not experienced. The research is sufficient to choose a provisional direction. It is not sufficient to claim a validated market, autonomous safety, or customer savings.
